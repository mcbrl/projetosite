On May 18th, the [Mind The Sec](http://mindthesec.com.br/riodejaneiro2017/) information security event took place in Rio de Janeiro . This event aims to exchange knowledge on the subject, explore market opportunities and trends, and foster relationships between companies and professionals in the field. It's not only for specialists but also for those directly or indirectly interested in cryptography, security, tools, and methodologies. The exchange of information happens with professionals who live and breathe security 24/7. The event was held at the Sheraton Grand Rio & Resort and boasted excellent infrastructure, dividing the lectures into 3 tracks.

The event opened with the following experts:

-  [Cassius Puodzius](https://www.linkedin.com/in/cassius-puodzius-a80a5525/?ppe=1) (ESET);
- [Thiago Marques](https://www.linkedin.com/in/thiagoolmarques/?ppe=1) (Karspersky);
- Fernando Mercês (Trend Micro).

The audience present at the security event had the opportunity to ask and present their questions regarding security, and of course the most debated topic at the event was the [WannaCry ransomware](https://www.wired.co.uk/article/wannacry-ransomware-virus-patch) phenomenon , which primarily threatened the corporate world. If you don't know what it is, [click here](http://www.techtudo.com.br/noticias/2017/05/wannacry-hackers-que-criaram-ransomware-tentam-reviver-ataque.ghtml) to learn more. At this meeting, the audience learned about the recommendations and measures taken to respond to the attack.

After the event opened, the tracks began, covering various themes simultaneously, addressing topics such as:

- The impacts of information security on Brazilian politics;
- Behavior and technical knowledge within corporations;
- Peculiarities of Brazilian Cybercrime;
- Inspiring people to build safety habits;
- Myths and facts about WannaCrypt;
- Increasing the level of security in communication between applications;
- Threat lifecycle management;
- There was also a lecture on cryptography (interestingly, it was titled "Leave Cryptography Alone!").

These were some of the topics that caught my attention the most, but without a doubt, the main star was WannaCry, which took center stage and was the subject of all the event's talks. Sometimes as the main topic, other times as a peripheral one.

### Part of the blame: IT professional

The point to reflect on is that WannaCry triggered an alert worldwide, demonstrating that information security and encryption need to be treated with seriousness and responsibility, and that this should not only be a matter for the private sector, but also for the public sector. Fernando Mercês' (Trend Micro) presentation, titled "Myths and Facts About WannaCry," focused on WannaCry and demonstrated how simple it is to investigate, highlighting the slight complexity of combining ransomware with an exploit and a worm. He showcased his laboratory, with a real infection, and demonstrated how Trend Micro detected it, allowing them to create a virus signature and develop a vaccine for the malware. When questioned about possible innovative measures to educate users on how to avoid such incidents, Mercês was emphatic in stating:

> This time the user is not at fault at all. If we had to hold anyone responsible, it would be the infrastructure technicians responsible for the updates who failed to deploy the patches that have been available since March.

With corrections and measures available since March 2017, the global scenario clearly demonstrates that companies still rely on misguided update practices, or even have little or no policy addressing information security, and this is dangerous and frightening, since we are talking about people who are in the IT field and should be concerned about data integrity.

### Part of the blame: End user

During the event, the user's responsibility in maintaining the security of their own information was also addressed. In Ramiro Pozzani's (SIDI – Samsung Institute for Information Technology Development) presentation, this was very clear, as, according to him, in most infection cases, the user's share of responsibility is extremely high. He also highlighted how inattention and carelessness can be dangerous, especially when it comes to allowing malicious or non-malicious programs to run on a PC. He gave an example of emails with links to banking websites that simulate the official page with an incredible level of perfection, managing to collect an alarming number of account numbers, passwords, and all the data necessary to make purchases using credit cards or even bank transfers.

The event achieved its goal, providing valuable knowledge, great opportunities, and strengthening relationships, bringing together professionals and enthusiasts, products and organizations. It was such a success that the date for next year's event has already been confirmed: May 17, 2018. Yes, it's definitely worth attending events like this.

### WannaCry

Formed by an ingeniously devastating mix of an exploit, worm, and ransomware, WannaCry spread like a plague, encrypting computers with the vulnerability worldwide. More than 200.000 computers in 150 countries were affected, with victims in various sectors, such as hospitals, banks, telecommunications companies, and retail stores.

After infecting its target, WannaCry continues to spread through local networks and the Internet. Even with the existence of a critical Microsoft patch (update package) since March 14, 2017, many companies and individuals in general did not install the update, leaving their systems vulnerable and making the spread of WannaCry much easier. Considering that there are still organizations using older systems such as Windows XP and Windows Server 2003 in production, they have become easy targets precisely because they lack any support from Microsoft.

It exploits a vulnerability in one of the oldest network protocols on the Internet, Server Message Block version 1 (SMBv1), which, ironically, remained active even without any use by computers. Combining all these factors, the result could only be one thing: a global outbreak of infection.

### McAfee

Unfortunately, one of the most anticipated guests, John McAfee, did not attend the event, controversially citing last-minute health problems. His presence would certainly have enriched the event, but the other speakers invited to replace him handled the task very well, and focused on what everyone wanted to see: the WannaCry outbreak.

One very interesting point was raised by the event's audience, with two very relevant questions:

1.  What would be the solution to avoid continuing to think reactively, that is, waiting for the system to get infected and experience all the trauma, only to then install a product that solves the problem?
2.  Wouldn't it be time to think about technology differently, in a proactive way?

These are objective questions, and the initial speakers who replaced John McAfee responded with unclear and evasive answers, stating that a problem like the one that occurred is inevitable, and the best way to adopt protective measures is to follow the manufacturer's instructions for the various applications.

### Targeted encryption

Cryptography could not be left out of an event of this magnitude. Therefore, in one of the last lectures of the day, [Diego Aranha](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dfaranha/?ppe=1) , a professor at the State University of Campinas (Unicamp), highlighted how important it is to fight for the freedom of individuals to use cryptography, as well as for companies in their applications, because without it all software would be completely vulnerable. Ahead of a meeting with the Supreme Federal Court (STF) for a public hearing on WhatsApp and the Brazilian Internet Bill of Rights (Marco Civil da Internet), he emphasized that some proposals from those who advocate for the control of cryptography would even make his work as a researcher impossible. The hearing was scheduled for June 2nd (Friday) and June 5th (Monday), from 9:00 AM to 12:30 PM and from 2:00 PM to 5:30 PM, on the 3rd floor of the STF.
