## DevOps

The concept of DevOps has many facets and can be explained in different ways in various sources. Some explain DevOps as collaborative processes between development and IT operations. Well, since this means absolutely nothing, let's try to look at its history. *To understand the gains of DevOps, it's important to understand the chaos that preceded it*.

[If you'd like to explore the concept in more depth, check out our content on What is DevOps!](/en/What-is-DevOps/)

In virtually all companies with a traditional IT structure, *sysadmins* (infrastructure/operations professionals) and developers not only form separate operational silos, but also strongly advocate diametrically opposed views on how IT should operate.

The reason is that developers are held accountable for delivering features, while infrastructure professionals are responsible for the stability and availability of systems. In traditional IT, changes are seen as risk factors, leading to the adoption of *processes that stifle deliveries, surrounding them with slow procedures and protocols* . To make matters worse, this stance finds ample support in outdated literature, which is still celebrated today by expensive consultants who have probably never delivered a single line of software in their lives (yes, this is for you, ITIL lawyer).

Developers vs. Operations: Neither is able to listen to the other, nor do they intend to. The **purpose** of DevOps is to end this. It is, above all, a new mental model, a **way of thinking** . Yes, ideas are powerful, but that doesn't mean much either. Let's talk about the *practice of DevOps*.

It is the practice of DevOps that will transform an ordinary company into a software company. And yes, every company is a software company (or must become one to survive). [*Software is eating the world*](https://a16z.com/2016/08/20/why-software-is-eating-the-world/) , and the graveyard is full of companies that were slow to understand this.

### **How does this practice materialize in everyday life?**

DevOps practices redefine *culture, tools, and processes* (now agile). This practice aims to improve the IT production process with more frequent and higher-quality deliveries.

DevOps starts from the understanding that suppressing change directly implies a greater accumulation of changes with each release. This leads to frequent and recurring errors and problems. Interestingly, the greatest risk lies in traditional risk management strategies, as these adopt practices that currently make no sense at all.

With this new approach, adopting DevOps means constantly seeking to automate repetitive processes throughout the entire software production chain. This implies self-service provisioning of resources for teams, automated build and testing pipelines, continuous delivery, tools that facilitate communication, collaboration and monitoring, among others.

*Adopting a DevOps culture is pointless with outdated tools.*

*It's no use buying tools without changing the culture.*

*Culture and tools are useless if processes remain archaic.*

This new mix of tools, culture, and processes grants teams great freedom to experiment with new technologies and platforms, while also enabling unified management of the entire production process. Containers, for example, have quickly become the ideal delivery model for public, private, or hybrid clouds, but are equally useful in building CI/CD pipelines and provisioning tools on workstations.

### Practice makes perfect!

DevOps practices transform the activity of developing and delivering technology within a company, generating fast workflows in tasks executed in a predictable manner.

In other words, productivity increases gradually as the practice matures, reducing the error rate and offering solutions to address any problems that may arise within a system. Some organizational boundaries in IT become blurred and/or reinvented, while ownership *of* deliverables gradually flows to those who have genuine expertise.

It's important to emphasize that there is no "magic box" of DevOps to be bought on the market, despite what the manufacturers of certain products claim. There are *excellent (if not the best) open-source and free tools* , with a business model that can be licensed later, when there is certainty that manufacturer support is desirable. There are also PaaS/SaaS tools whose initial adoption cost is essentially zero ( cheap or free entry-level *tiers* ), as well as specific offerings from each cloud provider.

## Docker

The adoption of DevOps can be accelerated when combined with the adoption of a containerization platform, such as Docker (Community Edition or Enterprise Edition). *This is actually excellent news for those who haven't yet transitioned to DevOps* – containers represent a shortcut to DevOps in several aspects.

Viewed from one angle, containers are a packaging technology that transforms the delivery into a self-contained *image* for software execution, ensuring its portability so that it runs in an isolated and predictable manner on any other server. This means that the software behaves as expected on the developer's workstation, in the build and test pipeline, in intermediate publishing environments, and in production.

This way, you save a lot on automation and delivery resources, and you reduce several traditional issues to common denominators (fault tolerance, scalability, monitoring, etc.).

## How DevOps can leverage your results.

DevOps practices are transformative: they change the perception of corporate IT from slow and reactive to fast and proactive. They allow IT to evolve into a business partner, not just a data center, where value delivery to customers is accelerated and results can be measured.

That's why DevOps is so important for your business. By automating your employees' repetitive tasks and measuring their results, you can achieve ever-increasing productivity in record time.

By combining an agile methodology with a focus on a stable technological environment, DevOps will certainly yield excellent results – just look at the example of large companies like Google, Facebook, and Amazon.
