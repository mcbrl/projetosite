The financial sector occupies a paradoxical position in the Brazilian corporate landscape. On the one hand, it is among those that invest the most in technology, with significant budgets allocated to innovation, security, and digitalization. On the other hand, it still grapples with operational bottlenecks that have persisted for years, often decades, rooted in manual processes, legacy systems, and organizational cultures resistant to change.

This paradox comes at a high cost. Not only financial, although that is the most visible, but also strategic. Institutions that fail to modernize their operations lose speed in competing, the ability to meet constantly evolving regulations, and, above all, the trust of customers who expect increasingly fluid and secure experiences.

The problem, in most cases, is not a lack of willingness to change. It's the difficulty in clearly identifying where the breaking points are and which technologies actually solve each of them. 

That is precisely the objective of this article: to map the ten biggest pain points in the financial sector, understand how each one manifests itself in the daily work of teams, and present the technological path to overcome them.

## **The 10 pain points of the financial sector and what technology does in each case.**

The following mapping is based on a simple principle: every operational pain point has an identifiable origin and a viable technological solution. What is lacking in most institutions is the combination of accurate diagnosis and structured implementation. This path becomes clearer in the following sections.

### **1\. Processes that are 100% manual and subject to human error.**

Of all the pain points in the financial sector, this is perhaps the oldest and most costly. Handwritten reconciliations, entries made one by one, approvals that depend on emails being sent to the right person at the right time. Each of these steps represents an open window for human error, and in the financial environment, errors have consequences that go far beyond rework.

The impact is not limited to data accuracy. Manual processes consume the time of skilled professionals on repetitive, low-value-added tasks, reduce the scalability of operations, and create dependency points that weaken the entire production chain. When a key employee is absent, the process stops.

Automating financial processes solves this bottleneck structurally. RPA (Robotic Process Automation) platforms, combined with API integrations, eliminate human intervention in repetitive steps and free up teams to focus on areas where judgment and analysis make a difference. The result is a faster, more reliable operation that is much better prepared for scaling.

### **2\. Parallel controls in outdated spreadsheets**

Few problems are as common and as underestimated as that of parallel spreadsheets. In virtually every medium-sized and large financial institution, it is possible to find different versions of the same report circulating between departments, each with slightly different data, updated at different times by different people.

This scenario, which often begins as a temporary solution, consolidates as an informal practice and starts dictating critical decisions based on information that may be days or weeks out of date. The risk is not only operational. In regulated environments, the absence of a single source of truth can compromise audits, reports to regulatory bodies, and the traceability required by **[standards such as the LGPD (](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2018/lei/l13709.htm)** Brazilian General Data Protection Law ).

The solution lies in adopting integrated platforms that centralize data in real time and establish a single source of truth for the entire organization. When systems communicate with each other through well-governed APIs, the parallel spreadsheet loses its reason for existing. The data arrives updated, in the right place, for those who need it.

### **3\. Lack of traceability and governance**

In a sector as regulated as finance, knowing what happened, when it happened, and who authorized each transaction is not just good practice; it's a requirement. However, many institutions still operate with processes in which the audit trail is fragmented, incomplete, or simply nonexistent.

This gap creates serious vulnerabilities. From a regulatory standpoint, the absence of traceability can result in penalties, bans, and reputational damage that is difficult to reverse. From an operational standpoint, it hinders the identification of failures, delays internal investigations, and increases response time to incidents.

Modern architectures based on managed APIs natively solve this problem. Platforms like **[Kong API Gateway](/en/A-more-efficient-public-sector-with-AI-and-APIs%3A-Kong-Gateway/)** log every call, every transaction, and every point of contact between systems, generating detailed logs that feed real-time observability dashboards. This visibility transforms governance from a reactive process into a continuous and proactive practice, aligned with the requirements of Open Finance and current regulations.

### **4\. Lack of standardization between teams**

When each area develops its own workflows, nomenclatures, and control criteria, the inevitable result is fragmentation. The same information receives different treatment depending on who processes it, which makes consolidation slower, analysis less reliable, and collaboration between teams much more difficult than it should be.

This problem is especially critical in institutions that have grown through acquisitions or that operate with multiple business units. A lack of financial standardization is not just a matter of internal organization. It directly affects the organization's ability to make quick and informed decisions, especially at times that require a consolidated perspective.

Technology solves this challenge through process governance. Platforms that define standardized workflows, validate data input, and centralize business rules ensure that all teams operate with the same criteria, regardless of where they are or which system they use. The practical effect is a more cohesive, more auditable operation that is much less dependent on the individual memory of each employee.

### **5\. Slow and inconsistent data consolidation**

Within the financial sector, closing the month is still a process that consumes days of intense work. Data is scattered across different systems, incompatible formats, and sources need to be manually reconciled before any analysis. The result arrives late, and when it does, it may already be outdated enough to compromise the quality of decisions.

This slowness has a significant strategic cost. In fast-moving markets, making decisions based on data from one or two weeks ago is the equivalent of driving while looking in the rearview mirror. The margin for error is high, and the ability to react to changing circumstances is proportionally reduced.

Real-time data integration, enabled by a well-structured API architecture, transforms this process. When systems are connected and data flows continuously between them, consolidation ceases to be a monthly event and becomes a permanent capability. Reports that previously took days to generate become available in minutes, with the reliability needed to confidently support strategic decisions.

### **6\. Dependence on IT for simple adjustments.**

Few situations generate as much friction in daily operations as excessive reliance on the IT department to resolve demands that, in theory, should be under the control of the business teams themselves. Changing a validation rule, adjusting an approval workflow, or adding a new field to a report are tasks that, in less modernized environments, require opening a support ticket, prioritization queues, and days of waiting.

This bottleneck is not the fault of IT. It is a consequence of rigid architectures, built without modularity, in which any adjustment, however small, affects critical layers of the system and requires specialized technical intervention. The side effect is twofold: business teams lose autonomy and agility, while IT becomes overwhelmed with operational demands that prevent it from focusing on strategic projects.

Low-code platforms and decoupled APIs structurally change this dynamic. When business rules are separated from the technical infrastructure, operational teams can make adjustments autonomously, without needing to involve the technology team with every change. IT, in turn, regains the capacity to act where its expertise truly makes a difference: in the evolution of the architecture and in product innovation.

### **7\. Difficulty in generating insights and simulating scenarios.**

Within the financial sector, many companies accumulate significant volumes of information in their systems, but still make decisions based on static reports, ad hoc analyses, and, not infrequently, the subjective experience of those in the room. The ability to cross-reference variables, simulate scenarios, and anticipate behaviors remains a privilege of the few.

This analytical limitation has well-defined technical roots. When data is fragmented into silos, when systems don't communicate, and when there's no observability layer to consolidate what's happening in real time, generating insights becomes a manual and time-consuming effort. The result is financial management that reacts to the past instead of anticipating the future.

The combination of modern data architecture, well-governed APIs, and analytics tools has transformed this scenario. With data flowing in a structured and continuous way between systems, it becomes possible to build dynamic dashboards, predictive models, and scenario simulations that put the management team in a much more proactive position. In regulated sectors, this analytical capability also feeds compliance reports with much greater accuracy and much less effort.

### **8\. Exposure to compliance and LGPD risks**

The regulatory environment of the Brazilian **[financial sector](/en/challenges-in-the-financial-sector%3A-how-Vertigo-technology-can-help./)** has never been more demanding. The combination of Central Bank guidelines, obligations imposed by the LGPD (Brazilian General Data Protection Law), and the new Open Finance rules has created a scenario in which compliance has ceased to be a one-off task and has become an ongoing responsibility. And for many institutions, the technological infrastructure simply hasn't kept pace.

The risks are real and growing. Data leaks, lack of documented consent, failures in anonymizing sensitive information, and processes without audit trails are vulnerabilities that can result in significant fines, regulatory sanctions, and reputational damage that is difficult to recover. The **[LGPD itself, in its article 52, provides for penalties of up to 2%](https://www.jusbrasil.com.br/topicos/233051473/paragrafo-7-artigo-52-da-lei-n-13709-de-14-de-agosto-de-2018)** of the organization's gross revenue, limited to fifty million reais per infraction. In a sector where trust is the most valuable asset, this type of exposure has consequences that go far beyond the financial balance sheet.

The technological solution lies in an architecture that treats compliance not as an additional layer, but as a native design requirement. This means API governance with granular access control, centrally applied security policies, auditable logs, and consent processes integrated into operational flows. When compliance is embedded in the architecture, the effort to maintain it decreases, and risk exposure consistently drops.

### **9\. High turnover and loss of tacit knowledge.**

The financial sector grapples with high turnover rates that pose a silent and persistent challenge when an experienced professional leaves, taking with them a body of knowledge that, more often than not, has never been documented. Business rules that exist only in the memory of the person who created them, exception flows that only that individual knows how to handle, decision criteria that have never been formalized in any system.

This problem worsens as the operation grows. In smaller teams, dependence on key personnel is manageable, albeit risky. In larger organizations, it becomes a real vector of operational instability. Each departure involves a costly transition period, a high risk of errors, and a loss of efficiency that rarely appears in reports, but which every manager recognizes when it happens.

Digitizing processes is the most effective answer to this challenge. When workflows are formalized on platforms, when business rules are documented in systems, and when decision criteria are codified in automations, knowledge ceases to be individual property and becomes the property of the organization. Turnover still exists, but its operational impact is significantly reduced.

### **10\. Lack of real-time visibility of cash flow.**

In the financial sector, making decisions about resource allocation, receivables financing, or liability coverage without an accurate and up-to-date view of cash flow is a risk that many institutions still run. Not through negligence, but because their systems simply weren't designed to offer this visibility continuously and reliably.

The problem has multiple origins. Accounts payable and receivable systems that don't communicate, delayed bank reconciliations, forecasts built on outdated data, and reports generated manually on a weekly or monthly basis. Each of these factors contributes to a fragmented view of the organization's true financial position, increasing exposure to liquidity risks and reducing the ability to act quickly in the face of changing circumstances.

Integrated dashboards, powered by APIs that connect financial, banking, and operational management systems in real time, definitively resolve this gap. With continuously updated cash position data accessible from any device, managers gain the clarity needed to make quick, informed decisions aligned with the current reality, not the reality of three days ago.

## **How each pain point translates into concrete financial loss.**

Analyzing the pain points of the financial sector in isolation is useful for diagnosis, but it is when the whole picture is observed that the true dimension of the problem becomes evident. Each of the ten bottlenecks described so far does not exist independently. They feed off each other, create cycles of inefficiency and, together, represent an operational cost that most institutions underestimate because they have never stopped to measure it.

Manual processes generate rework, and rework consumes the hours of professionals who could be dedicated to higher-value activities. Parallel spreadsheets produce inconsistencies, and inconsistencies delay decisions or, worse, justify wrong decisions. Lack of traceability exposes the institution to regulatory penalties that, in the case of the LGPD (Brazilian General Data Protection Law), can reach 2% of gross revenue, with a ceiling of fifty million reais per infraction. The absence of real-time visibility of cash flow has already forced solid companies to make misguided credit decisions during times of liquidity pressure.

Beyond the direct losses, there is an even more difficult cost to quantify: the opportunity cost. Every strategic project that doesn't move forward because IT is overwhelmed with operational demands, every product that reaches the market weeks after the ideal deadline because the systems don't integrate, every customer who migrates to a more agile competitor because the digital experience didn't meet expectations. These numbers don't appear on the income statement, but they are present in every decision that wasn't made in time.

The good news is that all these losses are avoidable. And the path to avoiding them starts in the same place: a structured technological architecture, governed and aligned with the specificities of a sector that operates under constant regulatory pressure.
