Few projects in the financial sector generate as much expectation and, at the same time, as much friction as technological modernization initiatives. The technical rationale for modernization is often clear: legacy systems increase maintenance costs, hinder integrations, limit the launch of new products, and expose the institution to growing regulatory risks. Even so, modernization projects are delayed, discontinued, or enter endless approval cycles.

The reason is almost never technology. It's internal resistance. Understanding where this resistance comes from, how it manifests itself, and, above all, how to overcome it in a structured way is what differentiates institutions that advance from those that remain trapped in architectures that no longer support their business objectives.

## **Where does the internal resistance to modernization come from?**

Resistance to technological modernization in the financial sector is not irrational. It has concrete roots, linked to past experiences with failed projects, organizational incentives that favor stability, and an often distorted understanding of the risks involved in change.

### **The burden of previous initiatives that failed to deliver.**

In many financial institutions, the institutional memory of previous modernization projects is negative. Systems that promised transformation and delivered complexity. Migrations that lasted years and cost twice as much as expected. Platforms implemented without adequate team preparation. Each of these episodes fuels legitimate skepticism and creates a high burden of proof for any new initiative. Overcoming this legacy requires not only a solid technical proposal, but a compelling, evidence-based risk management narrative.

### **Organizational incentives that encourage inertia.**

Legacy systems create specialists. Professionals who have dedicated years to mastering a specific technology or process have an interest, conscious or unconscious, in its continuity. Similarly, teams primarily evaluated by operational stability have little incentive to propose changes that, in the short term, always generate some level of disruption. This alignment of incentives is a powerful structural force and, when not explicitly addressed, tends to transform any modernization initiative into a project of passive resistance.

### **Distorted perception of the risk of change versus the risk of inaction.**

Perhaps the most persistent mechanism of resistance is the asymmetry in risk perception. The risk of modernizing is visible, tangible, and predictable: downtime during migration, learning curve for new tools, possibility of integration failures. The risk of not modernizing, on the other hand, is diffuse, cumulative, and usually materializes gradually: increased maintenance costs, loss of delivery speed, growing regulatory exposure, departure of talent frustrated with the available technology. When the two risks are placed side-by-side in a structured way, the equation almost always favors modernization. The problem is that this exercise is rarely done rigorously.

## **Effective strategies for overcoming resistance**

Recognizing the origins of resistance is the starting point. The next step is to build an approach that addresses each of these origins in a practical way, without ignoring the real risks or underestimating the organizational complexity involved.

### **Progressive modernization: iterating instead of replacing everything at once.**

The most effective strategy for simultaneously reducing resistance and operational risk is progressive modernization. Instead of proposing a complete and immediate replacement of critical systems, this approach creates layers of abstraction that allow legacy systems to coexist temporarily with modern architectures. [**Microservices and**](/en/AI-in-regulated-environments-such-as-APIs-ensures-innovation-without-compromising-compliance./) decoupled APIs enable this transition: each component can be modernized independently, with isolated and reversible impact. The progress accumulated over short cycles generates concrete evidence of success, which, in turn, reduces institutional skepticism.

### **Governance and communication as part of the methodology**

Modernization projects fail more often for organizational than technical reasons. The absence of a clear governance structure, with defined roles, transparent decision criteria, and regular communication mechanisms between IT and business areas, is one of the factors most correlated with failure. Similarly, initiatives that do not communicate their progress intelligibly to non-technical leaders quickly lose the executive support necessary to sustain the transformation. Therefore, communication is not a side activity to the project; it is part of the methodology.

### **Early involvement of affected teams**

Resistance is, in many cases, a response to exclusion. When the teams that will be impacted by a change are included in the diagnosis, prioritization, and validation of solutions, the level of resistance decreases considerably. Furthermore, the operational knowledge of these teams regarding existing processes is often indispensable for successful modernization. Treating teams as partners in the process, and not as passive recipients of change, is both a more effective management practice and a way to capture intelligence that systems do not document.

### **Structured business case: making the cost of inaction visible**

One of the most effective tools for overcoming the resistance of skeptical leaders is a structured business case. This means quantifying, with a clear methodology, the current cost of existing bottlenecks: hours of rework, the value of avoidable regulatory penalties, product launch time compared to more agile competitors, and the cost of maintaining legacy systems. When these numbers are placed alongside the investment needed for modernization, the decision shifts from a choice between risk and security to a choice between two types of risk, with distinct costs and probabilities.
