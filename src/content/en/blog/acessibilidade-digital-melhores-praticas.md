According to a report produced by We Are Social and Hootsuite in January 2021, there are currently 4,66 billion internet users worldwide. Since the global population is around 7,8 billion people, we can say that more than half of the world's population is connected to the internet.

This means that the internet is at the heart of work, learning, and entertainment processes. It is in this context that an important debate about Digital Accessibility is growing. But what does that mean?

Digital accessibility means eliminating barriers on the web. The term is based on the premise that websites and portals should be designed so that all people can perceive, understand, navigate, and interact effectively with the pages.

**In this article, produced by Neuber Guadelupe – UX Analyst at Vertigo, you will understand more about the need to create mechanisms that promote digital accessibility, as well as tips to make web browsing more inclusive.**

## **Digital Accessibility in Brazil** 

It is estimated that there are 45,6 million people with some type of disability in Brazil, which is equivalent to 25% of the population.

It is important to mention that, within the legal framework, Brazilian inclusion law ( [Law 13.146/2015](http://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2015/lei/l13146.htm) ) mandates that all Brazilian websites, public or private, must have tools that promote accessibility.

The Constitution provides for material equality among all, and it is the responsibility of those in government to ensure equal conditions so that people in special situations can carry out their activities like the rest of the population.

## **ESG and the new direction of the global market**

The acronym ESG stands for Environmental, Social, and Governance.

Promoting digital accessibility is an increasingly important issue for large companies, both in the public and private sectors.

Companies aligned with this movement are also involved in best environmental, social, and governance practices. These actions are becoming a major differentiator amidst the competition, and are a decisive factor in the decision-making of large clients and investors.

Despite the growing practice of ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance), many companies are still lagging behind and have not included these actions in their digital accessibility agendas. Even committed companies face difficulties in adapting their portals to be accessible to people with disabilities.

## **Web accessibility** 

When we talk about digital accessibility, it doesn't just involve people with disabilities. There are also cases of "situational" visual impairment, meaning people with presbyopia who may have difficulty filling out a form, for example, or even color blindness.

Paying attention to these details when developing a digital platform makes all the difference, because in addition to helping people with some type of limitation, it is also a relevant factor in search engine ranking criteria (SEO) such as Google.

To learn how to meet the requirements, you can access the [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)](https://www.w3.org/TR/2008/REC-WCAG20-20081211/).

## **Tools to make web browsing more inclusive.**

The internet is a vast repository of information. It's impossible to absorb all the new content created daily on the web. Now imagine if you have a disability? The level of difficulty increases somewhat, so some good practices can make this process more equitable. Check them out:

- **Jaws** – Paid screen reader. The license costs approximately R$8.000,00;
- **NVDA (No Video access)** Free screen reader;
- **Firefox** – 40% of users with disabilities prefer the browser;
- **Hand talk** – Plugin for translating content into sign language;
- **Essential Accessibility** – Assistive tool for various types of disabilities. Age, Stroke, Paralysis, Osteoarthritis;
- **BrowseAloud** It consists of a series of tools that add speech, reading, and translation;
- **DYNO Mapper** – Diagnostic tool;
- **WCAG ContrastChecker** – Tool for accessibility testing with background and foreground color.

## Here are some more possibilities for promoting digital accessibility.

- **Image descriptions –** It should contain a detailed description so that tools for visually impaired people can identify it;

- **Captcha –** If it's already complicated to understand what it means, imagine interpreting distorted letters for people who have some cognitive dysfunction. Today there are other types of solutions, with images and sound, for example;

- **Virtual keyboard –** It is very common for people who need to type in a bank password, for example. They are not recommended for people with Parkinson's disease or other physical disabilities;

- **Shortcut to content –** By pressing the "tab" key, the disabled person moves between the items and elements present. Now imagine moving between the elements and opening a pop-up. How does the disabled person return to the navigation? The recommended approach is to use buttons so that the person can directly access the content;

- **Keyboard shortcuts –** Allow shortcuts to facilitate accessibility;

- **Buttons with image text –** Buttons are fundamental elements for any strategy. Common mistakes include applying text only to images, without using alternative text. This makes conversion impossible for a PCD (Person with Disability).

- **Carousel –** According to the best practices of w3c.br, the use of advertisements, carousel photos, animated highlights, among others, is not prohibited, but the use of animations that are too fast and repeat indefinitely should be moderated.

## **Are you developing a website? Check out some inclusive practices below.** 

Developing websites that promote digital accessibility is a challenge for any good programmer. Fortunately, there is already a wealth of informative content that serves as a guide for designing a portal in a clear way for the largest possible number of people.

- When designing a website, tables need to follow a standard structure that includes: *table, summary, caption, thead, tbody* and if necessary, *tfoot*. For a **responsive table**use a class *table-responsive-vertical* within a *section*. ;

- **Labels –** It needs to have the "for" tag and its value will be the same as the one used in the input's "id". Inputs that are required must have *required*;

- **Anchors and Shortcuts –**  They assist people with disabilities in navigating the portal's content. *Go to content*.

- **H1, H2, H3 –** Users begin navigating between headings. In some cases, it's good practice to use H1 for logos in the header; however, when it comes to accessibility, we have to consider what makes the most sense for the user.

- **Long –** Always keep it in the language in which the portal is being created;

- **Alt –** The ALT attribute plays a very important role, as it allows visually impaired people to interpret an image and the description of audio content;

- **Title -** commonly used for describing logos within SVG tags;

- the tag It is used when a more detailed description of the image is needed, complementing the attribute. *old*.

## **Step-by-step guide to accessibility verification**

Want to know how to design your website in the best possible way while adhering to digital accessibility best practices? Check out how below:

1.  Validate the HTML by [W3C](https://validator.w3.org/)
2.  Has the page language been declared?
3.  Has the page contrast been validated (color blindness and color-by-color)?
4.  Does the page logo have a link?
5.  Is the first element of the header an h1 containing the page title?
6.  The remaining h1 tags on the page, if any, are within or ?
7.  Are h1-h6 arranged hierarchically?
8.  The image descriptions were made alt="" ?
9.  Can the website content be understood in the same way without the use of images?
10.  Is the focus styled in CSS for tab key navigation?
11.  Are all the links accessible via keyboard?
12.  Do the links include proper descriptions, without containing "click here" or "read more"?
13.  Do external links have any identifying marks that distinguish them?
14.  Do the downloadable files have any identifying marks that distinguish them?
15.  Do the downloadable files show a description and extension in the link text?
16.  Do the videos have subtitles and sign language?
17.  Does the video that starts automatically when you enter the page have a pause button?
18.  Are the labels and inputs related to each other?
19.  Are the required fields on the form properly marked?
20.  Do tables have thead, summary, caption, tbody, and tfoot to group content?
21.  Do the completed tasks include feedback or alerts about potential errors and what needs to be done?
22.  After all the necessary accessibility features were included, the code was validated using a validator. [W3C list](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ER/tools/)?
