Customer service carries an expectation that has grown faster than most operations can keep up with: immediate, contextualized responses without repetition of information, across any channel. Chatbots have solved part of this problem, but only the simplest part. The next step depends on a different architecture, capable of supporting autonomous agents with real-world context about the customer journey.

## **The structural limitations of traditional chatbots in customer service.**

Insurance companies that still operate customer service using a traditional chatbot layer face a well-known limitation: the system responds well to what has already been scripted, but fails as soon as the conversation leaves the decision tree. For the insured, this means repeating information, being transferred between channels, and frequently having to contact a human agent to resolve what should be trivial.

### **Fixed script flows and the volume of repetitive calls**

The fixed-rules chatbot handles basic, first-level questions well: policy status, duplicate payment slips, waiting periods. The problem arises with the volume of requests it **can't** handle: queries that depend on cross-referencing claims data with policy history, or that require minimal contextual decision-making. This excess leads to human calls, and the customer service operation grows proportionally to the volume of policies, not to the efficiency of the process. For the COO and the Director of Customer Experience, this is the invisible cost of the reactive chatbot: capacity that should scale with technology continues to scale with headcount.

### **Lack of context between channels and stages of the insured's journey.**

The second limitation is structural, not operational: the traditional chatbot treats each interaction as an isolated event. An insured customer who opens a claim through the app and then calls the call center often needs to recount the situation from scratch because the voice channel doesn't have access to what happened in the digital channel. This fragmentation reflects an architectural flaw: the channel systems don't share context with each other, and the chatbot inherits this limitation instead of resolving it.

## **What changes with an autonomous agent with context?**

The difference between a chatbot and an autonomous agent lies in their decision-making capacity. An autonomous agent operates in a goal-oriented manner: it receives a request, evaluates the available context, and decides on the next step, rather than following a fixed script of questions and answers.

### **Goal-oriented execution, not script-oriented execution.**

While the chatbot follows a predefined decision tree, the autonomous agent interprets the intent behind the request and chooses from multiple resolution paths. A request such as "I need to understand why my claim is stalled" no longer generates a generic response but triggers a real query to the process status, with a specific answer for that insured party.

### **Contextual memory throughout the journey**

The independent agent maintains a policy history, previous interactions, and claims status available at any point of contact. This context memory eliminates information repetition across channels and allows service to continue from where it left off, regardless of whether the insured initiated the conversation via the app, WhatsApp, or voice.

### **Orchestration between customer service, claims, and back office.**

Unlike a chatbot, which simply responds and ends the conversation, an autonomous agent orchestrates tasks between systems. It can open a ticket, update a record, or escalate a case to a human analyst with the complete context already attached, reducing handoff time and preventing the insured from having to repeat their story to each new interlocutor. This type of orchestration is already showing concrete results in **[claims settlement](/en/AI-agents-in-claims-settlement%3A-how-to-reduce-analysis-time-and-operational-costs./)** , where the reduction in analysis time depends directly on the quality of the context available to the agent.

## **The integration infrastructure behind the autonomous agent.**

No autonomous agent operates in isolation. The ability to make contextually sound decisions depends directly on the quality of integration between the systems that support insurance operations.

### **APIs, AI Gateway, and MCP as a real-time context layer.**

For an autonomous agent to access policy history, claims status, and back-office data in real time, these systems need to be exposed via well-managed APIs. An AI Gateway centralizes and controls this access, while the Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes how AI agents query different data sources without requiring point-to-point integration for each new system. This integration challenge intensifies in regulatory contexts such as **[Open Insurance](/en/open-insurance-AI-agents-regulatory-data/)** , which expands the volume of data exchanged between insurers, banks, and cooperatives and demands the same discipline of data exposure via API.

### **Governance and traceability of agent decisions.**

Autonomy without governance is an operational risk. Every decision made by an autonomous agent needs to be traceable: what data was consulted, what action was executed, and at what point the decision should have been escalated to a human. This traceability allows the compliance area to validate the agent's behavior and adjust autonomy limits as the operation matures.

## **From assisted automation to autonomous operation: a structured path.**

The transition from chatbot to autonomous agent doesn't happen in a single leap. It follows a progression of maturity, and skipping steps tends to generate more risk than gain.

### **Where does Vertigo's AI Journey connect to this advancement?**

Vertigo's AI Journey methodology structures this progression in five phases: Discover, Empower, Deploy, Sustain, and Intelligence. For customer service, this means mapping where the current chatbot is already at its limit (Discover), training teams and processes to operate with AI (Empower), putting the autonomous agent into production in a controlled manner (Deploy), sustaining the operation with continuous monitoring and adjustments (Sustain), and finally, extracting continuous intelligence about service patterns to anticipate customer needs (Intelligence).

### **How to assess the maturity of an insurance company before scaling independent agents.**

Before scaling an autonomous agent across the entire policyholder base, it's worth assessing the AI ​​maturity level of the operation: data quality, governance, and the teams' ability to support the operation, in addition to the available technology. A structured **[maturity diagnosis](/en/maturity-diagnosis-in-vertigo/)** prevents the insurer from investing in autonomy before having the integration and governance foundation that this autonomy requires.

## **What to prioritize to exit pilot mode and climb safely.**

Insurance companies that have already tested an autonomous agent pilot know that the challenge rarely lies in the AI ​​model itself. It lies in the integration with legacy systems, the quality of data exposed via API, and the clear definition of the agent's independent decision-making limits. Prioritizing this integration foundation before expanding the agent's scope is what separates a pilot that never scales from an operation that effectively reduces repetitive volume and improves the insured's experience. ## **FAQ**

**What differentiates a chatbot from an autonomous AI agent in customer service?**

- The chatbot follows a fixed script of questions and answers and fails outside of the expected script.
- The autonomous agent interprets the intent of the request, decides the next step, and maintains context across channels, reducing the volume of repetitive calls that currently overburden customer service operations.
- Source: Vertigo, **[AI Strategy & Operations](/en/strategy-tier-ai-strategy-operations/)**

**How does an independent agent access policy and claims data in real time?**

- Through well-managed APIs, an AI Gateway, and the Model Context Protocol (MCP), which standardize agent access to different systems without requiring point-to-point integration for each new piece of data.

**What risks does the autonomy of AI agents pose for the insurance company?**

- The main risk is operating autonomously without traceability: decisions that cannot be audited compromise compliance validation and make it difficult to adjust the agent's scope of action.
- Governance and traceability of decisions should accompany any scale of autonomy, not come after it.

**How do you assess whether an insurance company is ready to scale with independent agents?**

- Assessing data quality, governance, and the ability of teams to support operations, not just the technology available.
- A structured maturity assessment avoids investing in autonomy before having the necessary integration foundation.
- Source: Vertigo, **[AI Maturity Diagnosis](/en/maturity-diagnosis-in-vertigo/)**

**How long does it take to migrate from a traditional chatbot to an autonomous agent with context?**

- This is not a one-step migration. It follows the progression of the AI ​​Journey: Discover, Empower, Deploy, Sustain, and Intelligence, and the timeline varies according to the maturity and integration stage already existing in the insurance company.
