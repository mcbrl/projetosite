The Brazilian financial sector is undergoing a transformation that is neither linear nor gradual. It is simultaneous, structural, and, to a large extent, irreversible. The forces shaping this scenario—the consolidation of Open Finance, the accelerated adoption of artificial intelligence, the advancement of Banking as a Service (BaaS), and the intensification of regulatory pressure—do not operate in isolation. They combine and create an environment in which the rules of the game change while the game is still in progress.

For CFOs, CTOs, and operations leaders of financial institutions, understanding these trends is no longer a foresight exercise. It's a management requirement. The decisions made today about technology architecture, data governance, and systems integration will determine the competitive capacity of the coming seasons.

## **Open Finance: From regulatory compliance to competitive advantage**

Open Finance has ceased to be a regulatory obligation and has become a strategic variable. Institutions that treat its implementation as a compliance exercise are progressively discovering that the same API and data sharing structures can be used to create products and experiences that were previously unfeasible.

### **API infrastructure as a strategic asset**

Enabling for Open Finance required institutions to build or modernize their API layers. This investment, initially made with a regulatory focus, now creates a foundation that can be reused for a variety of initiatives: offering customization, **[partner integration](/en/Veecode-platform/)** , development of new channels, and expansion of embedded financial services. Institutions that understand this treat their API Management layer not as cost infrastructure, but as a growth platform.

### **Data portability as a driver of competition.**

Open Finance's data portability places customer experience at the heart of competitive battles. When customers can easily move their financial history between institutions, loyalty shifts from exit barriers to the quality of service delivered. For institutions still operating with fragmented processes and inconsistent digital experiences, this scenario represents a real risk of losing their customer base. For those with integrated architecture and data-driven personalization capabilities, it's a growth opportunity.

## **Artificial intelligence: from experimentation to operation**

[**Artificial intelligence**](/en/AI-in-regulated-environments-such-as-APIs-ensures-innovation-without-compromising-compliance./) has moved beyond being a roadmap topic and has become an operational reality in growing segments of the financial sector. However, the speed of this transition is not uniform. While some institutions are already reaping concrete results with AI in fraud detection, credit analysis, and automated customer service, others are still in the use case evaluation phase.

### **AI in fraud detection and risk management**

Among the use cases with the highest maturity and measurable return is fraud detection. Machine learning models trained with transactional data identify patterns of suspicious behavior with far greater accuracy than systems based on static rules. When combined with APIs that connect data from different sources, such as transactional history, location, and device behavior, these models can block fraud in real time, before the damage is done. Integration with relationship graphs between entities, additionally, allows the identification of fraud networks that escape the individual analysis of events.

### **Personalization at scale with generative AI and predictive models.**

The combination of behavioral data, financial history, and predictive models allows institutions to offer truly personalized recommendations, from the next most suitable product to the ideal timing for a credit offer. Generative AI technologies expand this capability by enabling the creation of conversational interactions that simulate human service with quality and uninterrupted availability. The impact is evident in both operational efficiency and customer experience.

### **Governance and ethics as operational requirements.**

The adoption of AI in the financial sector comes with increasing governance obligations. Automated decisions affecting credit, collections, or investment need to be explainable, auditable, and free from discriminatory bias. This means that the AI ​​strategy cannot be dissociated from the data strategy, the security architecture, and the regulatory compliance framework. Institutions that move forward with AI without this foundation run both regulatory and reputational risks.

## **Banking as a Service: The Financialization of Adjacent Sectors**

BaaS represents one of the most profound transformations in the structure of the financial sector. By allowing companies from other sectors to offer financial products under their own brand, supported by third-party banking infrastructure, BaaS redefines who can compete in the financial market and how.

### **New competitors and new business models**

Retailers, fintechs, e-commerce platforms, and technology companies are using BaaS to launch digital accounts, cards, credit, and insurance without needing to build the regulatory infrastructure of a bank. This creates competitive pressure on traditional financial institutions, which must simultaneously defend their customer base and assess whether it makes sense to position themselves as infrastructure providers for these new players.

### **Technical and regulatory qualification for BaaS**

Offering **[BaaS infrastructure](/en/Learn-about-serverless-BAAS-FAAS-and-container./)** requires a robust API architecture capable of supporting multiple partners with different integration needs, access levels, and security requirements. API governance, granular access control, and traceability of each transaction are non-negotiable requirements in this model. Institutions that have invested in modernizing their integration layer are therefore better positioned to explore BaaS as an additional revenue stream.

## **Regulation as an accelerating force, not a brake.**

One of the most relevant shifts in perspective in the financial sector is the growing understanding that regulation is not merely an operational constraint. When well integrated into the technological architecture, it becomes a competitive differentiator. Institutions that build compliance as a native requirement, rather than an additional layer, operate with less friction, less risk, and greater agility to innovate within regulatory boundaries.

In this context, the ability to respond quickly to new requirements from the Central Bank, SUSEP (the Brazilian insurance regulator), or the LGPD (Brazilian General Data Protection Law) depends directly on the quality of the underlying technological architecture. Legacy systems, manual processes, and point-to-point integrations make each new regulatory cycle a highly complex project. Modular architectures, with well-governed APIs and layers of abstraction between systems, transform this same response into a controlled and low-risk adaptation.
