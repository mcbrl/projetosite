The adoption of DevOps is accelerating significant transformations in the IT field. These changes are bringing success to technology professionals and companies.

With DevOps, we achieve higher frequency and quality in software deliveries, simpler and more agile processes ( *people over process* ), and modernization of the company without necessarily requiring large initial investments. Furthermore, it reduces friction between different IT departments that traditionally experience significant and recurring conflict with each release.

These are some of the advantages of implementing DevOps culture, tools, and processes. If you want to learn more about DevOps and the reason for its success, follow our article.

## **What is DevOps**

Let's start with a brief explanation of what DevOps is and what changes it brings to IT.

[You can find a more complete explanation of the subject here.](/en/What-is-DevOps/)

Traditionally, software engineering and IT infrastructure professionals belonged to distinct worlds (separate operational silos and diametrically opposed cultures). As a result, the routine of software delivery was a task surrounded by bureaucratic procedures based on obsolete risk management models. Consequently, there were recurring errors and rework, as well as delays and excessive deadlines, which were the norm in IT.

In summary: developers are held accountable for delivering features, and infrastructure professionals for the stability and availability of systems. In traditional IT, changes are seen as risk factors. This leads to the adoption of processes that *stifle* deliveries, surrounding them with slow procedures and protocols.

DevOps starts from the understanding that suppressing change directly implies a greater accumulation of changes with each delivery. This is the real risk factor! DevOps is a strategy that unites agile culture, tools, and processes. The practice aims to improve the IT production process with *more frequent and higher-quality deliveries*.

With this new approach, adopting DevOps means constantly seeking to automate repetitive processes throughout the entire software production chain. This implies self-service provisioning of resources for teams, automated build and testing pipelines, continuous delivery, tools that facilitate communication and collaboration, among others.

Interestingly, this new standardization grants teams considerable freedom to experiment with new technologies and platforms, while also enabling unified management of the entire production process.

Adopting DevOps is a long journey – believing there's a "magic box" of DevOps that can be acquired from an IT vendor is a mistake. The good news is that there are good, viable strategies that are practically "self-funded" – each step generates substantial savings that justify their continuation.

## **Common problems that cause DevOps to fail.**

To achieve good results with DevOps, it's necessary to be aware of potential obstacles. Check out the most common and harmful obstacles for your IT company below.

### **1: Erroneous automation**

Unlike companies that have been flirting with the DevOps model for longer, more traditional companies seeking improvement face difficulties. The problem is that they don't know exactly how or when to integrate new technologies.

The automation process in a company converging towards DevOps can take several months. This is precisely why questions arise about which strategy would be the most appropriate.

Keeping in mind an organized approach, highlighting priorities, can help solve this setback. The *most important thing is to understand that this is a long journey of constant learning* . It's essential to start with projects where multidisciplinary teams are motivated to take on the new responsibilities inherent in this model. For example, the team needs to be able to correctly provision the resources it needs. Furthermore, the team needs to know how to build its automated tests and monitor its application (or even instrument it correctly).

### **2: Understanding what continuous integration and continuous delivery are.**

Continuous integration is linked to source code changes. Code changes are sent to an automated build and test pipeline as soon as they are submitted to the central source repository. Continuous integration aims to find bugs faster and improve software quality.

Continuous delivery follows continuous integration by publishing the application with the changes to a real environment (production or pre-production). Continuous delivery may also be accompanied by other automated tests before making the application available for use.

Understanding these stages distinctly and the function of each one optimizes culture, tools, and processes.

### **3: Misalignment between Continuous Integration and Continuous Delivery.**

Keeping the definition of each in mind is important once again, since Continuous Integration feeds Continuous Delivery. For this reason, a complete automation pipeline, well-configured by the project team, is necessary.

The biggest challenge within these two complementary areas is empowering the teams to work towards both of these objectives.

### **4: Balancing Continuous Integration and Continuous Delivery with resource utilization**

For each change made to the code, the continuous integration pipeline can be triggered. This action can overload the environment that executes it. The same applies to the continuous delivery pipeline.

Increasing the workload unnecessarily can consume valuable resources and time. Adjusting the conveyor belt firing parameters is essential to achieving a good balance.

## **Planning is essential, but not excessively.**

Adopting DevOps is necessary to improve your production, streamlining delivery and automating resource provisioning. This increases your company's efficiency without necessarily investing heavily.

Planning the steps for adopting these practices is important, as is understanding that it's a long journey of constant learning, where many benefits are reaped along the way. Many vendors will present magical DevOps solutions packaged in beautiful packages, but the best tools are open source and can (and should!) be thoroughly tested before being eventually licensed.
