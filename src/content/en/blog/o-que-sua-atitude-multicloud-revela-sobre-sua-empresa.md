The multicloud model has been widely discussed recently. However, a surprising number of companies with regulatory constraints, which historically haven't had much in-house application development, are only now beginning to consider it.
Some of the reasons why people are thinking about using [multicloud](/en/multicloud-facing-the-challenge-of-managing-multiple-clouds/) Currently, this relates to the past of global IT: when Amazon EC2 was launched in 2006, the industry discovered it was possible to write software that controlled the entire infrastructure. Before, it was necessary to acquire multiple environments, open a support ticket, and wait a long time to obtain access that was still quite limited. It was this revolutionary change that created this enormous industry. [cloud-native](/en/Improving-cloud-native-application-delivery-with-the-Weave-Kubernetes-platform./) current.
But along the way, some of that excitement was lost. Some companies adopted the cloud without really knowing how to handle it. In their initial deployments, there were no roadmaps, which led them down certain paths that made it difficult to see business success, even causing costs to spiral out of control in unforeseen ways. Organizations expected to replace their data centers with the cloud and then discovered that the cloud was actually costing more. This is not an uncommon story at all.
Another problem is the difficulty in finding and hiring talent that understands the cloud. The responsibility is commonly passed on to infrastructure teams, who end up managing it in the same way they manage assets in the data center.
In other words, there's a lack of understanding of a fundamental point about the cloud: the assets are no longer physical, like hardware, racks, and cables. It's the software. Therefore, you need to take the same level of care with your cloud-native application as you do with your application software and the business applications you offer.
In summary, we previously had a new infrastructure that was adopted by a new set of consumers and digital businesses who saw many benefits, flexibility, and reduced operating costs. Currently, those migrating to the cloud are doing so without the necessary paradigm shift regarding infrastructure.
Cloud Native is essentially being used as a replacement for core data, with the same processes, practices, and cultures used in legacy systems. The result is that the challenges remain the same, but with greater friction and higher operating costs.
The issue is that many IT professionals still don't know exactly what multicloud can mean and what its benefits are. Furthermore, how people think about this topic often reflects how far they are on their journey to... ## What are you looking for when using multicloud?

Here are the most common thoughts that go through people's minds when they say that using multicloud will bring some problems:

1.  Having workloads that move back and forth between different cloud providers (usually based on some kind of cost-based optimization);
2.  Having workloads on different cloud providers to minimize dependence on any one cloud;
3.  Having workloads that are deployed in one cloud with backups in another;
4.  Having workloads that are deployed in one cloud and explode in another;
5.  Having workloads architected to exist across multiple cloud providers;
6.  Having different workloads on different cloud providers to take advantage of the unique features of various clouds or the familiarity of various teams with specific properties.

All of this seems reasonable, but it falls into one of the following categories: the first four thoughts are very cloud/infrastructure oriented and reflect considerations in private data centers. The last two are more Cloud 2.0; the applications look different because of the services that are available above the infrastructure layer in various clouds.

## What innovation are you leaving behind by thinking with a Cloud 1.0 mindset?

What becomes apparent is how conversations about the benefits of multicloud with more advanced organizations tend to focus more on the benefits of Cloud 2.0 than with organizations that lag behind.
A fair number of people still see the cloud (and, by extension, multicloud) as an infrastructure discussion focused solely on cost reduction. But, a *shift and lift* Moving to the cloud doesn't tend to reduce costs on its own.
Organizations that understand this issue are focusing on how to... [The cloud is an enabler.](/en/Transform-your-corporate-IT-into-an-innovation-enabler-with-cloud-native-technology./)They realize that some teams need different tools than others or, for some reason, have grown accustomed to a specific set of tools from one cloud rather than another.
In these cases, the conversation quickly shifts to: "How can I ensure that my staff receive what they need, but are still aware of its use and able to keep things safe and compliant?"
This means that, with organizations that derive the most value from the cloud (the leaders in Digital Transformation), the conversations are about how to embrace and manage heterogeneity in clouds, services, and applications. They are not referring to point-in-instance cost analyzers, implementation workload management mechanisms, or portability.
Many organizations will find that, even with a multi-cloud approach, most workloads gravitate toward one place. Others may find that multiple teams have an almost religious fervor surrounding their specific cloud choice. The important thing is that all these teams can get their work done.
If you are focused on [cost containment](/en/Continuous-integration-and-the-cost-challenges-in-the-cloud./)It might make sense to take a step back and ask questions about what else your organization could do. Cost is an important consideration in any business, but it doesn't exclude the rest.
There is a wealth of experience and interesting toolsets that can help simplify multicloud management for your organization. It's definitely possible to support the technologies your teams need and be confident in doing so.
