[One of the most popular technologies for solving system integration](/en/systems-integration) problems is ESB (Enterprise Service Bus). This technology operates at the service orchestration layer, in SOA (Service Oriented Architecture)-oriented architectures.

Although the trend is for this technology to gradually be replaced by the microservices model, ESB is far from being a legacy. On the contrary, ESB technologies continue to be the solution for the problems of many companies that need to integrate their internal systems at the bus layer.

For this reason, we wrote this article to shed light on what an ESB is and how this technology can help solve the integration problems of many companies.

## What is ESB?

An **Enterprise Service Bus (ESB)** is fundamentally an architecture, with a set of rules and principles for integrating numerous applications into a bus-like infrastructure.

There are numerous ESBs on the market that allow developers to use this type of architecture. However, the technologies vary in both functionality and usability.

The central idea of ​​an ESB is to integrate different applications by placing a communication bus between them. This allows applications to exchange messages with each other, regardless of their formats.

In this way, the systems communicate independently with each other, making the entire architecture more consistent, manageable, and secure.

The concept of ESB (Enterprise Support Branch) arose from the need to obtain an integration model that does not bring with it the problems generated by point-to-point integration; which is the type of integration in which applications communicate directly with each other.

Integration without the right tools is complicated. Many companies implement custom integration to solve the challenge of creating seamless connectivity. This method requires a developer to create **point-to-point integrations** between applications and services. As a business grows and the number of integrations increases, the point-to-point architecture or "spaghetti code" becomes complex, fragile, and expensive to maintain.

## What does an ESB tool do?

To better understand what an ESB tool does, we need to understand how an ESB architecture maps to the five fundamental principles of integration:

- **Orchestration**It brings together various components in the construction of a fundamental service. It is important to balance the "granularity" of services to promote reuse and increase resource management.
- **Transformation**Data transformation between canonical data formats and specific data formats required by each ESB connector. An example of this would be the transformation between CSV, Cobol copybook, or EDI formats to SOAP/XML or JSON. Canonical data formats can greatly simplify the transformation requirements associated with a large ESB implementation, where there are many consumers and providers, each with their own data formats and definitions.
- **Transport**: Transport protocol negotiation between various formats (such as HTTP, JMS, JDBC). Note: The [MuleSoft](/en/mulesoft) It treats databases as just another "service," turning JDBC into simply another transport (or terminal) where data can be accessed.
- **Mediation**To provide multiple interfaces for the purpose of:
    1.  To support multiple versions of a service for backward compatibility or alternatively;
    2.  Allow multiple channels for the same underlying component implementation.

Regarding mediation, this second requirement may involve providing multiple interfaces for the same component: a legacy interface (simple file) and a standards-compliant interface (SOAP/XML).

- **Non-functional consistency**For a typical ESB initiative, this might include consistency around how security and monitoring policies are applied and implemented. Furthermore, scalability and availability goals can be achieved by using multiple instances of an ESB to provide higher throughput (scalability) and eliminate single points of failure (SPOFs), which is the primary objective for highly available systems.

## Why is it impossible to talk about ESB without mentioning SOA?

SOA **(Service Oriented Architecture)** is closely connected to the emergence of ESB. To better understand this connection, it is necessary to better understand SOA and what motivated the development of ESB technology.

The development of systems in corporate environments has reached unpredictable proportions in the short term, due to the high complexity required for each element. Because of this disorganized growth, some small gaps have emerged.

Frequently, applications contained duplicate functionality, with the same code existing in several internal programs. For example, if several programs required credit check information, each of those programs would duplicate the code needed to perform the credit check.

These additional code blocks result in multiple inefficiencies, and furthermore, the code is rarely reused, leading to a waste of effort and money spent during development. As infrastructures become more complex, it becomes increasingly difficult for developers to maintain and support these applications. Companies lose agility.

Service-oriented architecture is the natural evolution of traditional systems architecture. This model addresses the needs for development and adaptation to the increasingly demanding new market requirements.

The megatrends of SaaS, mobile, and Big Data are converging to create a massive explosion of endpoints and data. In this new era, companies tend to be increasingly overwhelmed by connectivity demands, or they can seize the opportunity and gain **a competitive advantage** by seamlessly connecting everything.

Visionary companies are transforming this fragmentation into strength, connecting the current explosion of applications, data, partners, and customers into a single, high-performing entity.

In this sense, one of the most important components of SOA is the **Enterprise Service Bus – ESB** , a corporate service bus that makes system services more easily available to users and applications, accelerating integration processes.

## Why use an ESB?

Increasing the company's competitiveness and the productivity of IT teams is usually one of the direct consequences of adopting an ESB solution.

An ESB prevents developer rework, reduces system downtime, decreases troubleshooting time, and shortens the time to deliver new applications. An ESB helps monitor, maintain, and ensure application availability.

With all these benefits, many IT leaders are able to justify the ESB not as a cost, but as an investment, since it is possible to calculate the **positive ROI of the tool**.
