The Old Lady of Taubaté is a classic character created by Veríssimo at the end of the military dictatorship (during the Figueiredo government). Although old, the Old Lady is timeless – she was the last person in Brazil who still believed in the government, and therefore was constantly monitored by the Presidential Palace. In her nostalgic chronicles, our politicians breathed a sigh of relief when the Old Lady believed the denials issued after the scandals and blunders that are still so characteristic of our representatives today. If they lost the Old Lady, everything would be over; as long as she still believed, there would still be hope. In the same way, we will convince you that you need to adopt Docker now.
It's impossible not to think of the Old Lady when reading a typical IT audit report, even now that we're well into 2016. My impression is that IT auditors have restricted themselves to ITIL, PMP, Cobit certification literature and other similar rubbish, and have never kept up with anything that has happened in the last 10 years. When placed above the main obligation of IT – which is the pure and simple delivery of value – all this messianic baggage of "best practices" and "tools for excellence" has the same importance as Lair Ribeiro's Complete Self-Help Works: None.

## **"This is a management problem."**

It's typical among auditors and consultants to believe that: for a ship to sail a better course, we have to replace rowers with buglers. For them, everything is "a management problem." Technologies have only a marginal contribution to delivery processes, and processes are improved by becoming more bureaucratic and time-consuming. The golden dream of every auditor is to make IT as efficient as a government office in Sucupira.
We like to think of corporate IT as the engine of institutional innovation. But, more often than not, IT spends part of its time managing chaos and part trying to make its own life worse. We wasted a decade writing use cases as offerings to RUP. Until agile processes put an end to this insanity. Meanwhile, infrastructure teams built their castles (supported by "best practices" manuals). And now, delivering a *release* It's like shooting a fly in a dark room.

## **Control Freak**

Every organization has a story like this to tell. Some developer, with too many permissions and not enough caution, accidentally dropped a table in production. Formal approval processes are implemented for this type of operation so that it never happens again.
With the specialization of many IT areas, we have distinct teams for databases, networking, (*application servers, storage).* Each with its documented processes, success metrics, operational silos, ITIL-compliant processes, and obsession with illusions of control, has the opposite practical effect of what it intends. The forced division of roles and control mechanisms creates an abandonment of responsibility. In addition to harming communication between teams, it only ensures that deliverables lack quality.
ITIL (*Information Technology Infrastructure Library*ITIL has good adherence to operational processes associated with infrastructure and financial management. In these niches, the processes are still consistent. Still, we can find good quality. But, when we observe such processes from the perspective of value delivery, the whole thing starts to smell very bad. ITIL exudes the ancient stench of the ancestral depths of waterfall development (*waterfall model*).
*Digital companies* They know that the ability to innovate and deliver features quickly is a critical factor for success. In these organizations, delivering value has the appropriate priority for today's world. In "traditional" companies, delivery is seen as a risky act. The poor soul responsible for the task must now open formal requests to a dozen teams, hoping they will be executed in a timely and coordinated manner.
Nothing is more decisive in putting someone completely out of the competition than the inability to adapt quickly. *If current processes promote stability at the expense of deliverables, discard them and try again.* Repeat as many times as necessary until you learn to do it right.

## **Change Prevention**

One obvious fact that is obvious to anyone is that *compliance and confidentiality* And governance is, or should be, more concerned with gathering evidence of how agile processes are conducted than with imposing slow processes that seemed like a good idea in the 90s.
The distorted logic (which is the typical playbook of many auditors) is that every change represents a risk and must be analyzed. It also needs to be approved and implemented within a timeframe considered safe. IT is divided into operational silos (each with its own responsibilities, metrics, and SLAs). Formal processes are forced down the throats of each of its interfaces, and the value chain is disregarded. It's an absurd theater of enormous proportions, where everyone is happy with their metrics, but project delivery – which is the only value delivery that truly matters to the organization – becomes a cheerful walk through a minefield.
Since absolutely no one knows the real needs of the end-to-end delivery process, the approvals are *pro forma* (mere useless formality), the problems are many and recurring (cause and effect relationships are ignored) and the *troubleshooting* Anything is only feasible with a task force involving 4 or 5 distinct teams. Life becomes a Kafkaesque nightmare.

## **DevOps and ITIL**

No matter how many times you try until you succeed, your delivery processes will only be valuable when they support frequent deliveries. Preventing changes only guarantees that they will be held back, increasing the risk of each delivery. *How to ensure continuous deliveries are of high quality.* This is where efforts should be focused.

To the dismay of those who make a living from consulting, ITIL has failed miserably in its mission to bring efficiency to corporate IT. The DevOps model is a natural reaction to this failure. The success of cloud delivery adoption (and its low cost) is largely due to the fact that its processes solemnly ignored the ITIL model. And of all the technologies available for a corporate IT department to begin a transition to the DevOps model, Docker is by far the most convenient, efficient, and inexpensive.

## **Here we have Docker.**

Rarely do we have the privilege of witnessing such rapid and positive disruptions as the widespread adoption of *containers* . The simplicity of their use and the wealth of scenarios in which *containers* simplify everyone's lives represent a unique moment.

Before the **Docker** We lived in a world of two ITs: The world of **startups**, typically continuous delivery on cloud platforms, with agile processes. And the world of **Corporate IT,** The Kafkaesque nightmare we've already discussed, expensive and slow.
It's not that corporations ignored the existence of cloud offerings: moving email, CRM, and ERP to the cloud are obvious choices. However, when we talk about software delivery (which always exists, even if only for integration purposes), we become more concerned. *suites* It is in the cloud that we perceive these two opposing worlds. *And you always need to deliver software.*No matter how much your integrated logistics, CRM, or XPTO solution cost, if you deliver the end-user interface or neglect the necessary integrations, you'll be in trouble. It's not that platforms for building private clouds didn't exist. However, these represented a sudden disruption, without the possibility of gradual adoption and with a prohibitive learning curve.

## **Containers**

*Containers* They allow the same delivery model we have in the cloud to be replicated in the corporate environment very easily. The learning curve and the cost of adopting... *containers* They are minimal. In addition, there are plenty of options for a gradual transition in corporate IT. The flirtation with *containers* It immediately offers great possibilities without the need to set up a cloud or PaaS infrastructure. *upfront*.
The moment you realize that it's finally possible to conceive of a delivery that can rely on an immutable infrastructure – that's a moment…*take the red pill*".
Risks? When adopting *containers you* From day one, it will eliminate considerable risks – real elephants in the room – that corporate IT has become accustomed to turning a blind eye to.
Investment? Friends, we're already in the 21st century. Investments follow the perception of value – you will have... *containers* in production well before deciding to invest in tools that allow for economies of scale and better management.

## **Any road is a road.**

It's important to emphasize this: with very simple product "seams". *open source* It is possible for any organization to adopt delivery processes in a private cloud model without spending a single cent on licensing.
For the JEE world, *containers* They represent a safe escape route from the monolithic delivery model in *application servers* "Fat" for, for example, service deliveries implemented via *Spring boot*.
