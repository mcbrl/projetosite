Financial institutions and insurance companies have accelerated the adoption of artificial intelligence in recent technology investment cycles. Even so, few can answer with the same clarity how much value this investment has actually generated. Understanding this gap is the first step in transforming adoption into measurable results.

## **Why measuring AI adoption is not the same as measuring return on investment.**

Executive committees often track the volume of licenses, the number of [**agents in production**](/en/AI-agents-in-claims-settlement%3A-how-to-reduce-analysis-time-and-operational-costs./) , and the number of areas involved. These indicators show the speed of deployment, but they don't indicate whether AI is generating better decisions, faster processes, or avoided costs. Therefore, organizations that scale without this distinction tend to confuse movement with progress.

## **Which vanity metrics still dominate AI committees?**

Before delving deeper into the problem, it's worth understanding why these metrics have become standard. They are easy to collect and communicate progress in a simple way, which has made them attractive for executive reporting.

### **Active users and created agents do not indicate value generated.**

A high number of active users may reflect initial curiosity, not consistent and productive use. Similarly, counting created agents does not guarantee that they are sustainably solving business problems.

### **What these metrics hide about actual usage.**

While attention is focused on volume, essential questions remain unanswered, such as which use cases truly justify the investment and which should be reviewed or discontinued.

## **How can FinOps be applied to AI governance?**

FinOps has established itself as a discipline for providing control and predictability to cloud spending. Applying this same logic to **[AI helps financial institutions](/en/From-API-gateway-to-AI-gateway%3A-the-evolution-that-the-financial-sector-cannot-ignore./)** systematically connect cost and value, rather than treating the issue solely as a cost-cutting measure.

### **From cloud cost discipline to AI cost discipline.**

Just as happened with the cloud, the rapid expansion of AI without structured control tends to generate expenses that are difficult to justify. Bringing FinOps principles to this context means measuring return on investment per use case, and not just aggregate consumption.

### **Why the cost of a bad decision doesn't appear on the bill.**

The financial impact of a decision made using a poorly validated model typically doesn't show up in AI billing. It emerges indirectly in the business, making the root cause difficult to trace without a proper follow-up process.

## **What changes when there is a financial owner for an AI use case?**

Defining clear financial accountability for each AI initiative changes organizational behavior around it. When someone is accountable for the feedback on a specific use case, continuous review becomes part of the process, rather than dependent on isolated initiatives.

### **Clear accountability reduces portfolio inertia risk.**

Without a designated person responsible, use cases tend to remain active even when they no longer generate value, simply because deactivating them requires a decision that no one feels authorized to make.

### **Objective criteria for maintaining, reviewing, or discontinuing a use case.**

Establishing clear continuity criteria prevents the AI ​​portfolio from growing haphazardly and allows resources to be prioritized for initiatives that effectively support business results.

## **Where can financial institutions and insurance companies start?**

Structuring this governance doesn't require starting from scratch. The most efficient starting point is to understand, precisely, at what stage of maturity the organization is today.

### **Maturity diagnosis as a starting point**

Before defining financial governance processes, it is important to map existing gaps in how AI is adopted and monitored. This initial diagnosis guides which areas to prioritize first, avoiding scattered efforts.

### **From point-in-time governance to structured AI governance.**

Based on this mapping, it becomes possible to move from isolated control actions to a continuous governance model, in which each use case has an owner, evaluation criteria, and periodic review.
